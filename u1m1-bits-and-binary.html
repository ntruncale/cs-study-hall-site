A letter from Beijing

Context

Around 1700, the German mathematician Gottfried Leibniz invented a way to write every number using only two digits: 0 and 1. He wrote about it in letters to a friend in China, a French Jesuit missionary named Fr. Joachim Bouvet, S.J., who lived at the court of the Kangxi Emperor in Beijing.

Bouvet had been studying the I Ching, an ancient Chinese book. It contains 64 symbols called hexagrams, each a stack of six lines that are either solid or broken. Bouvet noticed something amazing. If a solid line means 1 and a broken line means 0, the hexagrams line up with Leibniz's binary numbers. He sent Leibniz a diagram, and in 1703 Leibniz published his famous paper on binary.

Two people from different countries and cultures shared what they knew, and saw something new. Three hundred years later, every computer on Earth runs on binary.

2

Three ideas

Experience

Idea 1: A bit is a tiny switch

Inside your iPad are billions of tiny electronic switches. Each one is either on (1) or off (0). One switch holds one bit, short for "binary digit." A group of 8 bits is called a byte.

Idea 2: Each place is worth double the one to its right

In normal numbers, places are worth 1, 10, 100, and so on. In binary, the places are worth 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, doubling each time. To read a binary number, add up the places that have a 1.

1286432168421
00001101
00001101 = 8 + 4 + 1 = 13

Idea 3: Bits mean whatever we agree they mean

The bits 01000001 are the number 65. But computers also agree that 65 stands for the letter A. In a picture, a 1 can mean "color this dot dark." The bits are the same. The code that people agree on gives them meaning.

Tip: Keep the place values handy: 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. You'll use them in almost every task today.
