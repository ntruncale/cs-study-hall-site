Alarms on the way to the Moon

Context

On July 20, 1969, minutes before Apollo 11 touched down on the Moon, alarms started flashing on the spacecraft's computer: 1202, then 1201. The computer had more work than it could handle.

The software had been designed for exactly this kind of moment. A team at MIT led by Margaret Hamilton had built it to put the most important jobs first. When the computer was overloaded, it dropped less important tasks and kept the landing calculations running. Mission Control said "Go," and the astronauts landed safely.

That software was an early example of what we now call an operating system: the layer that decides which jobs get the computer's attention. Hamilton helped make "software engineering" a respected field, and in 2016 she received the Presidential Medal of Freedom.

Three ideas

Experience

Idea 1: Hardware is the physical parts

Hardware is anything you could touch if you opened up the device.

  • Processor (CPU): the chip that does the calculations, billions per second.
  • Memory (RAM): fast, short-term workspace for whatever is running right now. It's erased when the power goes off.
  • Storage: long-term space for apps, photos and files. It keeps everything when the power is off.
  • Input and output: the touchscreen, keyboard, camera, microphone and speakers.

Memory and storage are measured in bytes. A kilobyte (KB) is about 1,000 bytes, a megabyte (MB) is about 1,000 KB, a gigabyte (GB) is about 1,000 MB, and a terabyte (TB) is about 1,000 GB.

Idea 2: Software is the instructions

Software is the programs that tell the hardware what to do. There are two main kinds:

  • The operating system (OS), like iPadOS, Windows or Android, manages the hardware. It decides which app gets the processor, memory, screen and speaker, and when.
  • Apps, like Safari, Google Docs or a game, do jobs for you. Apps ask the operating system whenever they need the hardware.

Idea 3: Each layer hides the details below it

When you tap Send, you don't think about electrical signals. The app doesn't control the Wi-Fi chip directly, either. It asks the operating system, and the OS handles the hardware. Hiding details like this is called abstraction. It's why the same app can run on many different devices.
