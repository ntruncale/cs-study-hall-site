How the Internet Moves Data

When you send a message, it doesn't travel in one piece. It's chopped into small packets that race across the world by different routes and get put back together at the other end.

The first message was "LO"

Context

On the night of October 29, 1969, a student programmer named Charley Kline sat at a computer at UCLA. He was trying to log in to another computer about 350 miles away, at the Stanford Research Institute, over a brand-new network called ARPANET.

He typed L. The other computer got it. He typed O. It got that too. Then he typed G, and the system crashed. So the first message ever sent over the network that grew into the internet was "LO," as in "lo and behold." About an hour later they fixed the problem and the login worked.

Today, the internet connects billions of devices. It still works on the same big idea that ARPANET was built on: split data into small packets and let them find their own way.

2

Three ideas

Experience

Idea 1: Data travels in packets

Your message, photo or video is cut into small pieces called packets. Each packet has a header, like the label on an envelope, and data, a small piece of what you sent.

to 142.250.72.14 · #1 of 3
See you at
to 142.250.72.14 · #2 of 3
the food
to 142.250.72.14 · #3 of 3
drive!

Idea 2: Addresses and routers

Every device on the internet has an IP address, a number like 142.250.72.14. Packets travel through routers, computers whose only job is to pass each packet one step closer to its address. Each step is called a hop. Packets from the same message can take different routes.

Idea 3: Put back together, and resent if lost

Packets can arrive out of order. The receiving computer uses the packet numbers to put them back in order. If a packet goes missing, the receiver asks for that one packet again, so the rest of the message doesn't have to be resent.

The internet has many paths between places. If a router breaks, packets simply go around it. That's what makes the internet reliable.
