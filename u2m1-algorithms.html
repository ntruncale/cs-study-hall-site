The calendar that skipped ten days

Context

By the 1500s, the calendar Europe had used since Julius Caesar had drifted about ten days off from the seasons. Easter was slowly sliding away from spring. In 1582, Pope Gregory XIII introduced a new calendar, and the next day after Thursday, October 4, was Friday, October 15.

A Jesuit mathematician named Fr. Christopher Clavius, S.J. was one of the leading experts behind the reform. He spent years explaining and defending its rules. At its heart is a short set of steps for deciding whether a year gets a February 29. That set of steps is an algorithm, and it's still running in your iPad's calendar today.

The word "algorithm" itself comes from the name of al-Khwarizmi, a Persian scholar who wrote about step-by-step methods for arithmetic in Baghdad around the year 820.

2

Three ideas

Experience

Idea 1: An algorithm is precise, ordered and finishes

An algorithm is a step-by-step set of instructions for solving a problem. A good one is precise (nobody has to guess what a step means), ordered (the steps happen in a clear sequence), and it finishes with an answer. "Add some sugar" isn't precise. "Add 2 teaspoons of sugar" is.

Idea 2: Three building blocks

Almost every algorithm is built from three things:

  • Sequence: do steps in order.
  • Selection: make a decision. "If this is true, do that."
  • Repetition: repeat steps. "Repeat until you're done."

Here is Clavius's leap year algorithm. It uses sequence and selection:

1. If the year divides evenly by 400, it IS a leap year. Stop.
2. Otherwise, if it divides evenly by 100, it is NOT a leap year. Stop.
3. Otherwise, if it divides evenly by 4, it IS a leap year. Stop.
4. Otherwise, it is NOT a leap year.

Idea 3: Break big problems into small ones

Big problems are easier to solve in pieces. Breaking a problem into smaller parts is called decomposition. "Run a food drive" is overwhelming. "Plan it, collect the food, deliver it" is three smaller problems you can solve one at a time.
