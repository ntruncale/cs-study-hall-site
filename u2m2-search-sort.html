Counting a whole country

Context

Every ten years, the United States counts every person living in the country. In 1880, clerks tallied the census by hand, and it took about eight years. The country was growing so fast that the next count might not finish before the one after it began.

A young engineer named Herman Hollerith had an idea: punch each person's answers as holes in a card, then let a machine read, count and sort the cards. With his machines, the basic count for the 1890 census took about one year.

Hollerith's company later became part of IBM, the same company that helped Fr. Busa with his index of Aquinas. Searching and sorting have been at the center of computing from the very beginning.

2

Three ideas

Experience

Idea 1: Linear search checks one at a time

Linear search starts at the beginning and checks each item until it finds what it wants. It works on any list, sorted or not. But for a list of 1,000 names, it might take 1,000 checks.

Idea 2: Binary search cuts the list in half

If a list is sorted, you can do much better. Binary search checks the middle item. If that's too big, the answer must be in the first half; if it's too small, the second half. Either way, half the list is gone after one check. Then it repeats with the half that's left.

Every check cuts the list in half, so binary search is incredibly fast:

  • 1,000 items: at most 10 checks
  • 1,000,000 items: at most 20 checks

Idea 3: Sorting makes searching fast

Binary search only works on sorted lists, so putting things in order matters. One simple sorting algorithm is selection sort: find the smallest item and swap it into the first spot. Then find the smallest of the rest and swap it into the second spot. Repeat until you reach the end.
