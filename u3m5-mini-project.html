Lending your eyes

Context

Hans Jørgen Wiberg, a Danish furniture maker, began losing his sight in his twenties. He noticed that blind and low-vision people often needed help with small things for just a minute: reading an expiration date, matching socks, finding the right bus.

In 2015 he and a small team launched an app called Be My Eyes. A person who needs help taps one button, and the app connects them by video to a sighted volunteer somewhere in the world who has a minute to spare. Millions of volunteers have signed up.

The app's code isn't magic. Underneath, it uses the same building blocks you've learned: it takes input, makes decisions, repeats steps and calls functions. What makes it special is that someone noticed a real person's need and built something to meet it.

How programmers plan

Experience

Idea 1: Start with the person

Before writing code, ask: Who is this for? What do they need? Then plan three things:

InputWhat information does the program need from the user?
ProcessWhat does it do with that information? Decisions? Repeats? Math?
OutputWhat does it show the user at the end?

Idea 2: Write pseudocode

Pseudocode is your plan written in plain language, step by step, before you write Python. It's your algorithm. "Ask for the price. If they paid enough, show the change. Otherwise, say they need more money."

Idea 3: Build a little, test a little

Don't write the whole program and then press Run. Write a few lines, run them, fix what's wrong, then add more. When it works, test it with edge cases: zero, the exact boundary, and answers the user might get wrong.

2. Bake Sale Cashier

For the service club volunteer at the register, who has to add up prices and make change quickly while a line forms.

InputHow many items; the price of each item (whole dollars); how much the customer paid
ProcessLoop to add up the prices; a function change_due(total, paid) that returns the change; a decision about whether they paid enough
OutputThe total, and either the change or "Not enough money"

2. Study Buddy Quiz

For a younger brother, sister or neighbor practicing for a test. You choose the subject and write at least 3 questions.

InputThe student's answer to each question
ProcessLists of questions and answers; a loop that asks each one; a decision that checks the answer and adds to the score; a function final_message(score) that returns encouragement
Output"Correct!" or the right answer after each question, then the score and an encouraging message

2. Step Tracker

For a grandparent whose doctor asked them to average 5,000 steps a day this week.

InputThe number of steps for each of 7 days
ProcessA loop for the 7 days with a running total; a function average(total, days) that returns the average (use //); a decision about whether the average is at least 5,000
OutputThe total, the average, and a message that's different if they met the goal
