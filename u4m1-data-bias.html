The nurse who used a chart to save lives

Context

During the Crimean War in the 1850s, Florence Nightingale led a team of nurses at a British army hospital. She kept careful records and noticed something shocking: far more soldiers were dying from diseases they caught in dirty, crowded hospitals than from their battle wounds.

Officials ignored tables of numbers, so in 1858 she drew a new kind of chart, now called a rose diagram, where the size of each wedge showed how many soldiers died each month and from what. The huge wedges for preventable disease were impossible to ignore. Her charts helped convince the government to clean up military hospitals, and the reforms saved many thousands of lives.

Nightingale was a pioneer of using data to care for people. She also knew data only tells the truth when it's collected and shown honestly.

2

Three ideas

Experience

Idea 1: Data is organized in tables

Data is information that's been collected: numbers, answers, measurements. It's usually stored in a table. Each row is one record (one person, one day, one donation), and each column is one kind of information about it.

Idea 2: Charts can tell the truth, or mislead

A chart can make a pattern obvious in a second. But a chart can also mislead. The most common trick is a bar chart whose axis doesn't start at zero, which makes tiny differences look enormous. Always check where the axis starts.

Idea 3: Who's in the data, and who's missing?

In 1936, a popular magazine called the Literary Digest mailed surveys to millions of Americans and got back about 2.4 million answers. It predicted that Alf Landon would beat President Franklin Roosevelt easily. Roosevelt won one of the biggest landslides in history.

What went wrong? Many of the names came from lists of car owners, telephone owners and magazine subscribers. During the Great Depression, those people tended to be wealthier than average, and the people who chose to mail back an answer weren't typical either. A huge survey of the wrong people is still wrong. This is called sampling bias: the people in the data don't represent the whole group.
