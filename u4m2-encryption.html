The codebreakers of Bletchley Park

Context

During World War II, the German military sent its radio messages scrambled by a machine called Enigma. Each day the settings changed, giving more than 150 million million million possible ways to scramble a message. The Germans believed it was unbreakable.

At Bletchley Park, a country estate in England, thousands of people worked in secret to break it. Mathematician Alan Turing designed a machine called the bombe, and the first one began working in 1940. It raced through Enigma settings far faster than any person could. About three out of four people working at Bletchley Park were women, who ran the machines, decoded messages and analyzed what they meant.

Many historians believe their work shortened the war and saved many lives. The workers kept their secret for decades, many of them for the rest of their lives. Today, encryption protects your messages, your passwords and your bank account, and the people trying to break it are criminals instead of armies.

Three ideas

Experience

Idea 1: Encryption uses a key

Encryption scrambles a readable message (the plaintext) into unreadable ciphertext. Only someone with the key can unscramble it. Remember from Unit 1 that packets pass through many routers on the way. Encryption means anyone who grabs a packet along the way sees only gibberish.

Idea 2: The Caesar cipher, and how to crack it

Julius Caesar reportedly protected messages by shifting every letter 3 places down the alphabet: A becomes D, B becomes E, and so on. The key is the number of places to shift. But there are only 25 possible keys, so an enemy can simply try them all. Trying every possible key is called a brute-force attack. Modern encryption uses keys with so many possibilities that trying them all would take longer than the age of the universe.

Idea 3: What makes a password strong

  • Length beats cleverness. Each extra character multiplies the number of guesses an attacker needs. A passphrase of four or five random words is long, strong and easy to remember.
  • Avoid the obvious. Attackers try common passwords, dictionary words, names, birthdays and simple swaps like "p@ssw0rd" first.
  • Never reuse a password. If one site is hacked, attackers try that same password everywhere else.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication. It asks for a second proof, like a code on your phone, so a stolen password alone isn't enough.

Build: your own cipher machine

Action
