The checkers program that beat its maker

Context

In the 1950s, an IBM engineer named Arthur Samuel wanted a computer to play checkers. He wasn't a great checkers player himself, so instead of writing down every rule for good play, he let the program learn. It played thousands of games, many against itself, and kept track of which kinds of positions tended to lead to wins.

The program improved until it could beat Samuel. In 1959 he described his work in a paper and used a new phrase for it: machine learning. A few years later his program won a game against a skilled human player, which made headlines.

Today's AI systems are enormously bigger, but they rest on Samuel's idea: instead of telling the computer exactly what to do, show it many examples and let it find the patterns.

2

Three ideas

Experience

Idea 1: Rules versus learning

In Unit 3 you wrote rules: if the age is under 12, print the child price. That works when people know the rules. But what's the rule for recognizing a cat in a photo, or telling spam from a real email? It's hard to write down. In machine learning, people give the computer many examples with the right answers, and the computer builds its own rules. The result is called a model.

Idea 2: Features, labels and training

Each example has features, the clues the model looks at, and a label, the right answer. A spam filter's features might be the words in an email; the label is "spam" or "not spam." Learning from labeled examples is called training, and the examples are the training data. After training, the model predicts labels for new examples it has never seen.

Idea 3: Models make mistakes

A model is never perfect. We measure it by its accuracy: the percent of predictions it gets right, especially on new data. And a model only knows what was in its training data. If the examples were narrow, wrong or biased, the model will be too.
