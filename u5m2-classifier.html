The computer that couldn't see her

Context

As a graduate student at MIT, Joy Buolamwini was building a project that used face-detection software. It worked for her lighter-skinned classmates, but it couldn't find her face, which is darker. It only detected her when she held up a plain white mask.

She decided to investigate. In 2018, she and computer scientist Timnit Gebru published a study called Gender Shades. They tested face-analysis systems from major tech companies on photos of people with a range of skin tones. For lighter-skinned men, the error rate was under 1%. For darker-skinned women, it was as high as about 35%.

The systems had learned mostly from photos of lighter-skinned faces. After the study, several companies improved their systems. Buolamwini founded the Algorithmic Justice League to push for AI that works fairly for everyone.

2

Three ideas

Experience

Idea 1: A classifier sorts things into categories

A classifier is a model that picks a category: spam or not spam, ripe or unripe, cat or dog. Each example is described by its features. With two features, you can draw each example as a dot on a graph.

Idea 2: Nearest neighbors vote

One simple classifier is called k-nearest neighbors. To classify a new example, it finds the k training examples closest to it on the graph, and they vote. If k is 3 and two of the three nearest examples are ripe, the new one is predicted ripe. It's like guessing someone's favorite team by asking their three closest neighbors.

Idea 3: Check accuracy for every group

A model can look great overall and still fail a smaller group, because the big group's results hide the small group's. That's exactly what Gender Shades found. Responsible builders test accuracy separately for each group of people the system will affect, and fix gaps by collecting representative training data.
