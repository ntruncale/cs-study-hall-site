The first actual bug

Context

On September 9, 1947, the team running the Harvard Mark II computer, a room-sized machine full of electrical switches, couldn't figure out why it was giving wrong answers. They finally opened it up and found the problem: a moth stuck in one of the switches.

They carefully removed it and taped it into the logbook with a note: "First actual case of bug being found." Grace Hopper, whom you met in the Functions module, worked with that team and loved telling the story. Engineers had called problems "bugs" for decades, but the moth made the word famous. You can still see the logbook page at the Smithsonian.

Today, you'll find and fix bugs too. They won't have wings, but the process is the same: notice something is wrong, look closely, figure out the cause, fix it, and test again.

How to build and debug

Experience

Idea 1: Build in small steps

Follow your pseudocode one line at a time. After every few lines, tap Run. If you write 30 lines before running anything, you'll have 30 places to look for a bug.

Idea 2: When there's an error

Read the message, especially the line number. Look at that line and the line above it. Python fixes one error at a time, so after a fix, run again to find the next one.

Idea 3: When it runs but gives the wrong answer

  • Add a print to see what's inside a variable: print("score is", score). Delete it when you're done.
  • Rubber duck it: explain your code out loud, line by line, to a helper, a friend, or even a rubber duck. You'll often hear yourself find the bug.
  • Test edge cases: zero, the exact boundary, all right answers, all wrong answers.
