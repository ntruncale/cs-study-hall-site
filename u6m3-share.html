Men and women for others

Context

On August 6, 1945, a young Spanish Jesuit named Pedro Arrupe was living just outside Hiroshima, Japan, when the atomic bomb fell. He had studied medicine before becoming a priest, so he turned the Jesuit house into a makeshift hospital and cared for dozens of injured survivors.

Years later, as the leader of all the Jesuits worldwide, Fr. Arrupe gave a famous speech to graduates of Jesuit schools in Valencia, Spain, in 1973. He said the goal of Jesuit education is to form "men for others": people who use what they've learned not for themselves alone, but in service of others, especially the poor. Today Jesuit schools say "men and women for others."

That's what your capstone has been about. You learned how computers, algorithms, code, data and AI work. Now the question is what you'll do with them, and for whom.

Testing, improving and sharing

Experience

Idea 1: Watch someone use it

Hand your iPad to a classmate, a senior helper or (best of all) the person you built it for. Don't explain anything. Just watch where they hesitate or get confused. Then ask for feedback with three prompts:

I like…what works well
I wish…what was confusing or missing
What if…an idea to make it better

Idea 2: Real software is never finished

Apps you use get updated constantly because their makers keep listening to users. Making a change, testing it, and repeating is called iteration. One small improvement based on real feedback is worth more than ten features nobody asked for.

Idea 3: A short pitch

When you share a project, keep it short: the problem (who needs help and why), your solution (what your program does), and what's next (how you'd improve it with more time).
